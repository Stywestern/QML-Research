## Core Concepts
### 1. Multi-Dimensional Definition of Quantum Advantage
* Quantum advantage must be evaluated across multiple dimensions rather than exclusively by wall-clock time (`time-to-solution`).
* Key metrics include:
  * **Solution quality:** Proximity to the global optimum.
  * **Solution diversity:** Ability to generate multiple distinct near-optimal configurations.
  * **Cost-to-solution:** Overall resource expenditure required.
* Early paradigms (Grover's search, standard quantum annealing, QAOA) provide a foundation but require refinement across complexity theory, algorithmic design, and benchmarking to achieve practical utility.
### 2. Hardness of Approximation & Complexity Classes
* In many real-world settings, exact solutions are unnecessary; acceptable approximations allow for potential exponential speedups.
* Optimization problem complexity hierarchy:
  * **APX:** Problems admitting polynomial-time approximation algorithms with constant bounded approximation ratios.
  * **PTAS:** Problems admitting polynomial-time algorithms guaranteed to find solutions within a factor of $1 + \varepsilon$ of the optimum ($\varepsilon > 0$).
  * **FPTAS:** A restricted subclass of PTAS where runtime is polynomial in both input size and $1/\varepsilon$.
* Quantum algorithms offer potential new approaches or improvements over existing classical approximation schemes.
### 3. Role of Quantum Heuristics
* Due to unknown spectral gap scaling and hardware noise, quantum annealing and variational approaches function primarily as **heuristics** (algorithms without provable performance or runtime guarantees).
* The success of classical heuristic approaches motivates the study of quantum heuristics to solve complex combinatorial problems where classical methods scale poorly.
## Complexity & Approximation Hierarchy
### 1. PO (Polynomial-time Optimization)
* **Definition:** The optimization counterpart to **P**. Problems solvable to exact optimality in polynomial time relative to input size.
* **Examples:** Shortest Path (Dijkstra), Minimum Spanning Tree (Prim/Kruskal).
* **Use Cases:** Deterministic network routing and flow optimization.
### 2. NPO (Non-deterministic Polynomial-time Optimization)
* **Definition:** The optimization counterpart to **NP**. Candidate solutions are verifiable in polynomial time, but finding exact optima is typically NP-hard.
* **Examples:** Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP), Unit Commitment Problem (UCP).
* **Use Cases:** Power grid scheduling, logistics, and supply chain optimization.
### 3. APX (Approximable)
* **Definition:** A subclass of NPO problems admitting polynomial-time approximation algorithms with a constant worst-case approximation ratio bounded by a constant $c$.
* **Examples:** Metric TSP (Christofides' algorithm), Vertex Cover.
* **Use Cases:** Large-scale network design and facility location where bounded worst-case suboptimality is acceptable.
### 4. PTAS (Polynomial-Time Approximation Scheme)
* **Definition:** Family of algorithms parameterized by an accuracy target $\varepsilon > 0$, guaranteeing a solution within a factor of $1 \pm \varepsilon$ of the optimum in polynomial time relative to input size $n$.
* **Caveat:** Runtime exponent may depend exponentially on $1/\varepsilon$ (e.g., $O(n^{1/\varepsilon})$).
* **Examples:** Euclidean TSP (Arora's algorithm).
* **Use Cases:** Geometric packing and spatial resource allocation with tunable precision.
### 5. FPTAS (Fully Polynomial-Time Approximation Scheme)
* **Definition:** Strictest approximation tier. Running time is polynomial in **both** input size $n$ and the inverse precision parameter $1/\varepsilon$.
* **Examples:** 0-1 Knapsack Problem, Subset Sum Problem.
* **Use Cases:** Financial budgeting and capital allocation requiring high precision at large scales without exponential runtime penalties.

## Quantum Annealing and Adiabatic Evolution
* **Mechanism:** Continuous-time evolution interpolating between an initial mixing Hamiltonian (uniform superposition) and a problem Hamiltonian (classical Ising model).
* **Spectral Gap Bottleneck:** Exact adiabatic runtime is proportional to the inverse square of the minimum energy gap ($\Delta g$) in the eigenspectrum.
* **Complexity Implication:** For APX-hard problems, the minimum gap shrinks exponentially with problem size $n$. Thus, exact quantum annealing is intractable in the worst case, shifting research toward empirical performance analysis in ideal and noisy settings.

# Grover Search, QPE, and Gibbs Sampling in Quantum Optimization
## 1. Grover Search & Function Minimization
* **Mechanism:** Provides a quadratic query/minimization speedup over classical brute-force search.
* **Practical Limitations:** The advantage is relative to brute-force search; in many practical problem classes where advanced classical algorithms exist, the effective speedup is less pronounced.
* **Hybrid Utility:** Can be combined with classical or other quantum algorithms (e.g., dynamic programming acceleration) to achieve overall quadratic speedups in specific domains.
## 2. Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)
* **Mechanism:** Estimates eigenphases of unitary operators to compute matrix eigenvalues and solve Hamiltonian-related problems.
* **Significance:** Forms the basis of exponential speedups (e.g., Shor's algorithm) and applies to optimization when problems are encoded into non-diagonal Hamiltonians.
* **Hardware Requirement:** Heavily dependent on fault-tolerant quantum computing architecture.
## 3. Quantum Gibbs Sampling
* **Mechanism:** Generates sample sequences from complex probability distributions (extending classical MCMC methods used in constraint satisfaction).
* **Complexity:** Believed to be at least **QMA-hard** (Quantum Merlin-Arthur, containing P and NP).
* **Quantum Advantage:** Potential speedups arise because specific Gibbs states can be prepared more efficiently on quantum systems than via classical simulation under certain conditions.

# Quantum Imaginary Time Evolution & Alternative Cost Functions
## 1. Quantum Imaginary Time Evolution (QITE)
* **Mechanism:** Simulates non-unitary imaginary-time evolution ($\tau = it$) starting from an initial state $\vert\psi_0\rangle$ with non-zero ground-state overlap.
* **Effect:** Exponentially amplifies ground-state amplitudes while suppressing excited states.
* **Limitations & Capability:** Because imaginary time evolution is non-unitary, it cannot be implemented directly on standard quantum hardware without heuristic approximations (e.g., local state-updates). Theoretically enables solving QMA-hard problems like generic ground-state search and Gibbs state preparation.
## 2. Alternative Cost Functions for Noisy Quantum Algorithms
* **Motivation:** Standard mean-value cost functions in Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) are vulnerable to noise and barren plateaus. Alternative formulations bias optimization toward superior sample subsets:
  * **Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR):** Sorts measurement samples by objective value and averages only over the best $\alpha$-fraction ($\alpha \in [0, 1]$).
  * **Gibbs Objective Function:** Biases optimization estimates by applying weighted exponential penalties that emphasize high-performing samples over poor ones.

# Convex Optimization, Linear Programming, and SDP in Quantum Computing 

## 1. Query Complexity and General Convex Optimization
* **No Universal Speedup:** In general black-box settings with gradient access or oracle access (membership, separation, optimization), quantum lower bounds frequently match classical bounds.
* **Scaling Behavior:** Solutions scale either with polynomial dependence on inverse precision $1/\varepsilon$ (independent of dimension $n$) or with polynomial $n$-dependence and polylogarithmic $\varepsilon$-dependence (via methods like the ellipsoid algorithm). Only mild quantum speedups or no-go results exist in this general setting.
## 2. Structured Convex Optimization: LP and SDP
* **Linear Programming (LP) & Semidefinite Programming (SDP):** SDP minimizes a linear trace objective subject to linear inequality constraints over positive semidefinite matrices ($\mathbf{X} \succcurlyeq 0$). LP is a special case where all input matrices are diagonal.
* **The Precision vs. Dimension Trade-Off:** Quantum solvers for LP and SDP achieve better scaling with respect to dimension $n$ and the number of constraints $m$, but suffer from a **worse dependence on precision $\varepsilon$**.

# Quantum Speedups
## 1. Quantum IPMs for Linear Programming
* **Condition-Number Independence:** Recent quantum IPM algorithms achieve speedups for linear programs without introducing dependence on a condition number.
* **Problem Regime ($m \gg n$):** Effective when the number of linear inequalities $m$ is much larger than the number of variables $n$.
* **Scaling Comparison:** 
  * *Classical Solver:* $\tilde{O}(mn + n^{2.5})$
  * *Quantum Analogue:* $m \cdot \text{poly}(n, \log(1/\varepsilon))$
* **Dependency:** Requires **QRAM** access to achieve this asymptotic advantage.
## 2. Quantum Dynamical Systems & Central Path Tracking
* **Mechanism:** Formulates quantum algorithms via a Schrödinger equation whose trajectory follows a gradient descent or central path.
* **Capabilities:** Provides convergence guarantees for convex and non-convex problems (noting potential exponential time for general non-convex cases).
* **Key Advantage:** Bypasses the need for QRAM while offering superior runtimes across most parameters compared to standard quantum IPM formulations.
## 3. Hybrid Dynamic Programming and Grover Search
* **Mechanism:** Classical dynamic programming computes partial solutions over an exponentially large table (Boolean hypercube $\{0,1\}^n$), followed by Grover's search to find the optimal global combination.
* **Example (TSP):** Computes shortest paths through up to 24% of cities classically, then uses Grover search to assemble the complete tour.
* **Target Problem Types:** Vertex-ordering problems (TSP) and set-partitioning problems (graph coloring, minimum Steiner tree, tree width, single-machine scheduling).
## 4. Scaling & Speedup Bounds
* **Overall Speedup:** Typically **subquadratic** (at most quadratic in generic hypercube formulations) due to the overhead of computing the initial classical DP table.
* **Open Problem:** It remains an open question whether quantum speedups are achievable for dynamic programming algorithms utilizing table structures outside the Boolean hypercube format.

# HJB Equations, Dynamic Programming, and Quantum Reinforcement Learning
## 1. The HJB Equation and Curse of Dimensionality
* **Intractability:** Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equations and discrete Bellman equations suffer severely from the curse of dimensionality, becoming intractable for modest dimensions ($n \ge 10$).
* **Scope:** Underpins optimal control, state estimation, discrete optimization, and reinforcement learning (RL).
## 2. Quantum Reinforcement Learning (QRL) Access Models & Speedups
* **Coherent / Quantum Environment Access:**
  * Requires unitary encoding of environment transition functions.
  * Yields **quadratic speedups** in policy optimization for regularized policies (such as those defined by parameterized quantum circuits). Lower bounds prove these quadratic bounds are optimal in general settings.
  * Exponential speedups are achievable only in contrived, unrealistic environments.
* **Classical Environment Access:**
  * When environment interaction is restricted to classical access, provable super-polynomial advantages exist for quantum RL algorithms under cryptographic assumptions (e.g., hardness of the discrete logarithm problem).
# Problem Pre-Processing, Decomposition, and Quantum Hardware Paradigms 
## 1. Pre-Processing and QUBO Reduction
* **Variable Elimination:** Effective pre-processing can remove up to 95% of variables in problems like Euclidean TSP and Steiner tree.
* **QUBO Reductions:** Analytic reduction rules allow conditional fixing of variables while preserving at least one optimal solution.
* **Sparsity & Independence:** Matrix sparsity determines variable correlation; uncorrelated groups can be decoupled into independent sub-QUBOs.
## 2. Problem Decomposition
* **Purpose:** Enables mapping large optimization instances onto hardware-limited quantum processors.
* **Trade-Offs:** Introduces trade-offs between subproblem size/count and global solution quality. Quantum advantage on subproblems is necessary but not sufficient for global advantage.

# Strategic Directions Toward Quantum Advantage & Conclusion 
## 1. Three Core Research Directions
* **Direction 1: Real-World Application Benchmarking**
  * Identify genuine problem instances where classical solvers struggle (e.g., sustainable energy transitions, financial asset allocation).
  * Rigorously define objective functions and manage modeling assumptions (such as linearization impact).
* **Direction 2: Application-Agnostic & Heuristic Discovery**
  * Use handcrafted or artificial instances to discover quantum heuristics and new algorithmic primitives.
  * Exponential speedups via reduction classes are generally confined to $\text{NP} \cap \text{BQP}$.
* **Direction 3: Theoretical Depth & Beyond-Quadratic Speedups**
  * Expand understanding beyond worst-case complexity to average-case analysis.
  * Shift theoretical design away from unrealistic assumptions like QRAM and target speedups exceeding quadratic scaling.
## 2. Outlook and Co-Design
* **Theory + Empiricism Synergy:** Combining theoretical guarantees with empirical testing on gate-based systems (hundreds of qubits) is critical for hardware co-design and validating heuristic behavior in noisy settings.

“The key problem associated with the HJB equation, continuous or discrete, is the ‘curse of dimensionality’. Solving equation (12) by conventional methods of numerical analysis quickly becomes intractable even in rather modest dimensions (for example, if x(t) ∈ Rn for n ≥ 10). In other words, a direct approach to solving the HJB equation has slim chances of succeeding classically. In addition to optimal control, the HJB equation has many applications: from nonlinear filtering or state estimation for differential equations148 to discrete optimization — the most relevant application in the context of this Review. For instance, the HJB equation for discrete-time dynamics can be seen as the famous Bellman equation of dynamic programming, which in turn connects HJB equations with reinforcement learning, a family of algorithms in which an agent aims to learn optimal decisions in unknown environments through the experience of taking actions and observing the rewards gained (see the Supplementary information for details). Quantum approaches can be classified based on whether the learning agent has a standard classical access to the environment or whether the interaction itself is quantum. In the latter case, numerous types of quantum oracular access to the task environment have been explored: for example, where coherent access to a unitary encoding the transition function is possible149, or where the environment closely mimics classical reinforcement-learning settings, allows only actions as inputs and has memory150. The main conceptual limitations are the requirement to coherently access the task environment, which might only be possible in very restricted or constructed cases. In some works, the focus was also on identifying the exact solutions, for which, in real-world problems, the search spaces are simply too large. The up-side of the aforementioned results is that the speedups concern the number of interactions with the environment and are not contingent on any assumptions in complexity theory. It has been shown151 that quadratic speedups in policy optimization are possible as long as the policies satisfy certain regularity conditions — which are, for example, satisfied for policies defined by parameterized quantum circuits. The lower bounds for this problem have been established for a more general setting152 and they show that these quadratic improvements cannot be improved further. However, if the environments are not arbitrary but special, more substantial improvements are possible, including provable exponential speedups153. The contrived nature of these environments makes these results unlikely to have implications in practice. When the access to the task environment is restricted to be classical, provable speedups are possible too. For example, it has been proved154,155 that there exists task environments with a super-polynomial advantage for quantum reinforcement-learning algorithms (with respect to finding optimal policies and optimal q-functions), unless the discrete logarithm problem has an efficient classical solution.” ([Abbas et al., 2024, p. 725](zotero://select/library/items/8GWMT3M9)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/9EG2IQ4U?page=9&annotation=UECNI3ME))

“How pre-processing is implemented in practice depends on the data, the optimization algorithm and its mathematical formulation and on the computational platform. In fact, it is not uncommon for many problems — such as the Euclidean TSP, or Steiner tree problems in graphs — that pre-processing is able to remove 95% of the variables and, hence, substantially reduce the problem size and resource requirements179,180. Considering the example of QUBO, various reduction techniques to eliminate optimization variables have been suggested in the literature and are reviewed in ref. 180. For example, analytic reduction rules181 make it possible to conditionally fix certain optimization variables, while guaranteeing that at least one optimal solution will remain in the search space. In addition to the number of optimization variables, their degree of correlation (which is determined by the sparsity of the QUBO matrix) is also of interest. In the case that certain groups of decision variables are not correlated, QUBOs can be decomposed trivially into smaller sub-QUBOs to be solved independently. More generally, decomposition approaches can be an option to break large problems into smaller subproblems. Decomposition is well established in classical optimization but can also help quantum optimization — for example, in fitting a large problem on a smaller quantum computer. However, decomposition often introduces a trade-off between the size and number of subproblems and the overall resulting solution quality. Thus, achieving a quantum advantage in the subproblems is necessary but not sufficient for achieving a quantum advantage for the global problem. But as long as the advantage can be carried over, decomposition can be a key tool towards quantum advantage. The development of quantum-specific decomposition schemes might further help achieve this goal.” ([Abbas et al., 2024, p. 727](zotero://select/library/items/8GWMT3M9)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/9EG2IQ4U?page=11&annotation=LNF3FZRG))

“In digital quantum computing187, the basic units of information are qubits instead of bits. The information processing consists of consecutive quantum gate operations such as CNOT gates and single-qubit rotation gates, which, together, form a quantum circuit, similar to a classical logical circuit. Important bottlenecks of existing digital quantum computers are limited coherence times, which result in limited circuit depths, limited qubit numbers and limited qubit connectivity. There are various ways to quantify the performance of a digital quantum computer. An overview of best practices for executing quantum optimization algorithms on noisy digital quantum hardware is given in the Supplementary information. Analog quantum computing hardware is a more specialized kind of quantum computer that performs continuous time evolution of the Schrödinger equation188. These types of computers are a natural fit for quantum simulation applications188–196. Additionally, the quantum annealing algorithm (discussed earlier) provides a way for these computers to be used for unconstrained optimization tasks as well2,3,167,197,198. Thus far, analog quantum computers have been developed for solving optimization problems in the format of Ising models199 and special cases of the maximum independent set200. In contrast with digital quantum computing, which is very flexible, analog quantum computing focuses on a more limited scope of optimization tasks, compatible with analog hardware.” ([Abbas et al., 2024, p. 728](zotero://select/library/items/8GWMT3M9)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/9EG2IQ4U?page=12&annotation=7G3JMAPT))

“By contrast, quantum algorithms are primarily developed theoretically owing to limited hardware availability. However, this is changing. The rise of gate-based quantum computers with hundreds of qubits enables complex circuits beyond exact classical simulability220,221. To develop and improve quantum optimization algorithms, in addition to continued technological progress, it is crucial to combine theoretical and empirical research — similar to classical algorithms. In this context, we believe that there are three key directions to advance on the path towards quantum advantage. First, real-world optimization problems should be identified that are promising candidates to demonstrate quantum advantage. This is challenging owing to the many open questions about the future performance of quantum optimization algorithms, their heuristic nature and the overhead introduced by quantum error correction. Additionally, these problems must be truly challenging for classical solvers; if classical solvers are sufficient, there is no margin for quantum advantage. A formal understanding of the actual goal of application is required. This may sound trivial, but practical challenges can arise when trying to formally define objective functions, constraints or preferences in a rigorous mathematical way, or when trying to get the required data. Furthermore, as mentioned earlier, problems are typically modelled to be solvable, often involving simplifications such as linearization. Although the impact of these simplifications can be negligible in some cases, it might be notable in terms of practical impact or solvability in others. Challenging and relevant problems must be rigorously defined, ideally in a model-independent way, and necessary data should be available to construct instances using different modelling approaches. Once a concrete problem instance is identified, it should be compared with the state-of-the-art classical algorithms. Two illustrative applications (discussed further in the Supplementary information) are financial asset allocation and the sustainable energy transition: although these may not be the first domains to benefit from quantum advantage in optimization, they are well suited to illustrate different levels of modelling complexity, data requirements and the scale needed for real-world applications. The resulting problems, including all introduced features, can be difficult to solve classically. Such a playground to construct real-world motivated benchmarking problems with increasingly complex features is essential to test algorithms and track progress from toy examples towards practically relevant problems. The second direction involves finding application-agnostic (possibly hand-crafted) optimization problem instances and the corresponding (heuristic) quantum algorithms that can outperform the best classical algorithms. If (artificial) instances are found where quantum algorithms excel, it might be then possible to discover related applications or understand the source of the advantage and extend it to broader classes of problems or algorithms. These instances may even motivate completely new algorithmic primitives. An example in which quantum algorithms might achieve a provably exponential speedup over the best-known classical algorithms is optimization problems, which are reductions (for example, factoring problems222,223). However, such examples are unlikely to occur in practice other than by encoding problems that are in NP ∩ BQP. Here, we suggest an empirical approach to identify more generally applicable quantum heuristics and less formal problem structures in which these heuristics have an advantage in certain instances. For the third direction, theoretical understanding of the potential and the limitations of quantum optimization algorithms should be enhanced, and new algorithms developed that ideally achieve more than a quadratic speedup. This includes research on exact algorithms, approximations and heuristics, as well as answering more general questions such as about the potential of quantum optimization in the average case in contrast to the worst case. Theoretical insights help to identify promising directions for empirical testing and to explore scenarios that cannot be tested empirically yet. For instance, to enable practical advantages, there should be a move away from relying on QRAM for designing quantum optimization algorithms with rigorous quantum speedups: although the QRAM model has led to elegant results, its physical realization remains a distant possibility at best. Furthermore, finding quantum speedups beyond quadratic is crucial for practical quantum advantage224. New theories can inspire new algorithms that address this difficult task, unlock new problem classes and create opportunities to find problem instances that may benefit from quantum optimization. To conclude, use of quantum hardware is already critical in accelerating progress towards quantum advantage. Simultaneously, state-of-the-art classical optimization must be leveraged to identify where better solutions are needed. Empirical studies do not replace theoretical research, but rather they complement each other. Outlook We have provided a comprehensive overview of the potential, the challenges and the emerging research areas in quantum optimization. We observed that although complexity theory is useful as a guide to provable performance guarantees, it may not always be useful for finding practical quantum advantage. This underscores the need to develop and analyse quantum heuristics to augment theoretical research. We have highlighted key problem classes in optimization, reviewed existing algorithms and suggested new research directions. We have also discussed the importance of benchmarking for identifying quantum advantage. As quantum hardware evolves, a new era is beginning in which progress in quantum algorithms comes from both theoretical analysis and empirical methods. There is a strong need to continue discovering new theories and algorithms with provable performance guarantees, but there must also be increased development of quantum heuristics and benchmarking of their performance on real quantum computers. The improving quantum capabilities unlock opportunities to further advance quantum optimization (including the co-design of quantum algorithms that account for hardware limitations, discussed in theSupplementary information). Even if there is no immediate practical quantum advantage, the intuition gained from running experiments on real hardware will at least enable quick validation of proposals and may also inspire new theoretical research. In addition to technical advances in quantum optimization, there is also the question of responsible use of this new technology. Future research should be done with the awareness that optimization use cases occur in a multitude of contexts — some with positive societal impact, others with nefarious intent. We hope that open scientific discussions will encourage the advance of quantum optimization and we advocate responsible use of the insights presented in this article. Where applications of quantum optimization algorithms are chosen and funded, we encourage the prioritization of those with positive social impact. Quantum optimization holds vast potential for various applications, but challenges remain in demonstrating practical quantum advantage. When a tangible quantum advantage is demonstrated, we expect that quantum optimization will rapidly influence many domains, given the widespread relevance of optimization.” ([Abbas et al., 2024, p. 729](zotero://select/library/items/8GWMT3M9)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/9EG2IQ4U?page=13&annotation=XTI5Y5MV))
