
Combinatorial AC Power Flow: Discretization Strategy 
* Solves continuous AC Power Flow on Ising/QUBO hardware by assigning **1 spin decision variable per voltage component**: $$\mu_i = \mu_0^i + s_i^\mu \Delta \mu_i, \quad s_i^\mu \in \{\pm 1\}$$
* Step size $\Delta \mu$ decays exponentially ($\Delta \mu: 0.1 \to 10^{-4}$), zooming in from coarse searching to fine convergence. 
* Minimizes sum of squared active/reactive power mismatches: $$\min_{\mathbf{s}} \sum (P_{\text{calc}} - P_{\text{target}})^2 + (Q_{\text{calc}} - Q_{\text{target}})^2 \implies \text{Ising } H = \sum h_i s_i + \sum J_{ij} s_i s_j$$
Models Tested & Comparison:
* **Models Tested:** The paper evaluated three distinct solvers on a 4-bus AC Power Flow problem: QAOA (a gate-based approach run on a software simulator with 8 qubits). D-Wave QA (a physical quantum annealer using 26 variables) , and Fujitsu QIIO (a specialized classical digital annealing CMOS chip using 20 variables).
* **Accuracy & Convergence:** Both Fujitsu QIIO and D-Wave QA successfully matched ground-truth classical Newton-Raphson solutions down to $10^{-3}$ precision. QIIO converged the fastest in just 63 iterations, while D-Wave QA converged in 222 iterations
* **Performance Trade-Offs:** Simulated QAOA was the slowest and failed to hit the convergence threshold within 300 iterations. While D-Wave achieved blazingly fast QPU computation time ($0.015\text{s}$ per iteration), it suffered from network/embedding wall-clock latency ($1.25\text{s}$ per iteration). Overall, physical/digital annealers significantly outperformed gate-based QAOA, which was hindered by high compilation times and classical optimization overhead.

Discretizing Continuous Variables for Quantum Annealers (The Kaseb Trick) 
* **The Problem:** Continuous power grid mathematics relies on precise floating-point decimals, but Quantum Annealers (Ising Models) only understand binary spins ($+1$ or $-1$). 
* **The Old Approach (Inefficient):** Representing a precise voltage (e.g., 1.045) required mapping it to long binary strings, which ate up dozens of precious qubits per grid node. 
The Solution: Iterative Base-Value Refinement Instead of calculating the exact decimal all at once, the algorithm splits the work between classical and quantum hardware: 
1. **Single-Qubit Direction Finding:** Use only 1 qubit per voltage value. The classical computer stores the current guess ($\mu_{old}$) and asks the quantum computer one simple question: *"Should I move slightly higher (+1) or slightly lower (-1)?"*
2. **Exponentially Shrinking Step Size ($\Delta$):** The system transitions from a coarse global search to ultra-fine precision by exponentially shrinking the step size on the classical side after every single iteration.
	* **Iteration 1:** Massive jumps ($\Delta = 0.1$) 
	* *Iteration 50:* Micro-refinements ($\Delta = 0.001$)
3. Squaring the Error (Ising Mapping): The grid equations are subtracted from their targets to find the residual error, which is then squared. Squaring these terms naturally produces a quadratic equation that maps perfectly to an Ising Hamiltonian. Finding the lowest physical "energy" state automatically finds the lowest power grid error.

Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA):
* **Core Definition:** QAOA is the gate-based quantum computing (GQC) equivalent to Quantum Annealing. It is a hybrid Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC) designed specifically to solve discrete combinatorial optimization problems. 
1. The Goal: Solving QUBOs on Gate-Based Hardware 
	* Like Quantum Annealing, QAOA solves optimization problems formatted as a QUBO or Ising Model. 
	* Instead of physically freezing a cryogenic chip into its ground state (like D-Wave), QAOA uses standard digital quantum logic gates on a universal quantum computer to digitally simulate that process.
2. Core Mechanism: Alternating Hamiltonians 
	 * QAOA searches for the optimal solution by alternating between two parameterized layers of quantum gates: 
	 * **Cost Hamiltonian (The Grader):** Encodes the actual objective function of the combinatorial problem. It assigns penalties to bad answers and rewards to good ones. 
	 * **Mixer Hamiltonian (The Shuffler):** Drives transitions between computational basis states to ensure the algorithm explores the full solution space, preventing it from getting trapped in a single local minimum. 
	 * **The Hybrid Loop:** By alternating these layers and using a classical optimizer to tweak the gate parameters, the circuit prepares a quantum state that has a high probability of collapsing into the optimal solution when measured. 
3. The Big Trade-Off: Depth vs. Circuit Complexity 
	* **Algorithm Depth ($p$):** Controls how many times the layers are repeated. 
	* **The Theory:** Higher depth mathematically improves the approximation and probability of finding the perfect answer. 
	* **The NISQ Reality:** Increasing depth also increases circuit complexity. On current noisy hardware, deeper circuits introduce more physical thermal noise and require more parameters, which makes convergence much harder for the classical optimizer.
4. QAOA vs. AQC (Why use QAOA instead of Annealers?) 
	* **The AQC Limitation:** Physical annealers like D-Wave suffer from **Embedding Restrictions**. Their chips have fixed physical wires, requiring you to waste dozens of extra "chain" qubits just to build bridges across the chip to connect distant variables. 
	* **The QAOA Advantage:** Because QAOA uses software logic gates, it is highly flexible. You can encode arbitrary interactions—and even higher-order polynomial interactions—without being restricted by physical hardware graphs. 
	* **The Catch:** QAOA's success depends critically on both the fidelity of the gate-based hardware (to avoid noise) and the power of the classical optimizer (like Adam) to tune the parameters.

Execution Details: QAOA vs. Quantum Annealing 
1. QAOA Execution (The Variational Loop) 
	* **The Process:** QAOA does not find the answer in a single run. It relies on a classical-quantum feedback loop. 
	* **The Ansatz & Measurement:** The quantum computer prepares a parameterized state (the ansatz) and measures the expected cost of that state. 
	* **The Classical Optimizer:** A classical optimizer (like Adam) takes that cost, updates the gate parameters, and reruns the circuit. In this experiment, 100 classical optimization steps were required per iteration. 
2. Quantum Annealing Execution (Hardware Constraints) Unlike QAOA (which is simulated or uses flexible gates), physical Quantum Annealers face hardware wiring constraints that introduce specific physical overheads: 
	* **Minor Embedding:** Physical qubits have fixed, limited connections. To solve a highly connected problem, multiple physical qubits must be grouped together to represent a single mathematical variable. 
	* **Chain Strength:** The magnetic force used to bind those grouped qubits together. 
	* It must be tuned perfectly: too weak, and the chain "breaks" (qubits in the same group yield different answers). Too strong, and it artificially overpowers the actual objective function of the problem. 
	* **Probabilistic Readout:** Due to quantum noise and thermal fluctuations, annealers are run multiple times per step (e.g., 1,000 readouts). The solver simply selects the sample that settled into the lowest energy state


Results & Performance: QAOA vs. Quantum Annealing vs. Digital Annealing
1. Hardware Footprint & Compilation 
	* **QAOA:** Required 8 qubits but suffered from massive compilation/transpilation overhead compared to the annealers. 
	* **D-Wave (QA):** Required 26 spin variables. The high number is due to PyQUBO generating extra variables to reduce higher-order grid math into quadratic terms. 
	* **Fujitsu QIIO:** Used 20 decision variables with fast compilation. 
2. Convergence Speed (The Iteration Race) 
	* **QIIO (Digital Annealer):** Fastest convergence (**63 iterations**) at $0.06\text{s}$ per step. 
	* **D-Wave QA:** Converged in **222 iterations**. 
	* *The Bottleneck:* While QPU calculation time was blazingly fast ($0.015\text{s}$), the actual wall-clock time was $1.25\text{s}$ per step due to cloud communication, sampling, and minor embedding overhead. It also oscillated significantly before stabilizing. 
	* **QAOA (Gate-Based):** Slowest method. Maxed out at **300 iterations** without reaching the $10^{-3}$ convergence threshold. 
3. Solution Accuracy 
	* **AQC / Digital:** Both QA and QIIO successfully matched the classical Newton-Raphson (NR) exact solutions to $10^{-3}$ precision for both real ($\mu$) and imaginary ($\omega$) voltage components. 
	* **QAOA:** Achieved comparable accuracy for real voltage ($\mu$) but struggled with larger deviations in the imaginary phase ($\omega$).

“3. Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm QAOA belongs to the GQC paradigm and is designed for solving combinatorial optimization problems. QAOA encodes problems as an Ising model or an equivalent QUBO, and the goal is to minimize a cost Hamiltonian defined over spin/binary variables. QAOA uses a parameterized sequence of discrete quantum gates that alternate between applying a cost Hamiltonian and a mixer Hamiltonian. Note that the cost Hamiltonian encodes the objective function of the given combinatorial optimization problem, while the mixer Hamiltonian drives transitions between computational basis states to ensure exploration of the solution space rather than getting stuck in a single configuration. By optimizing 4https://pyqubo.readthedocs.io the gate parameters classically, the algorithm prepares a quantum state with a high probability of yielding near-optimal solutions when measured. The algorithm’s depth controls the trade-off between solution quality and circuit complexity. While a higher depth can improve approximations, it also increases circuit complexity and may hinder the convergence of the classical optimizer. QAOA has been experimentally demonstrated on near-term GQC hardware, such as superconducting qubit platforms (e.g., Weidenfeller, Valor, Gacon, Tornow, Bello, Woerner and Egger (2022)). While current devices are constrained by the number of qubits and gate fidelity, they are flexible in encoding an arbitrary cost Hamiltonian without the embedding restrictions of AQC. In principle, higher-order interactions can be incorporated into the cost Hamiltonian, though often at the expense of additional qubits or circuit depth. The performance of QAOA depends critically on both the quality of the quantum hardware and the classical optimizer that tunes the variational parameters to maximize the solution probability.” ([Kaseb et al., 2026, p. 5](zotero://select/library/items/II9MFBJE)) ([pdf](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/PNYRLMDY?page=6&annotation=T3ZEFBUS))
