
# The Gap in Existing Literature
## Hardware Constraints & Past Limitations
Older QA hardware suffered from low qubit counts and poor topological connectivity. This forced past researchers to limit their benchmarks to:
- Low-dimensional matrices (e.g., 35 to 100 variables).
- Highly sparse QUBO configurations (e.g., 5,000 variables but only 40,000 quadratic terms).
## The Resulting Misconception
Because past benchmarks used these simplified constraints, they concluded that QA offers no significant advantage over—and sometimes loses to—classical solvers (e.g., Integer Programming, Simulated Annealing). 

# Advancements in QA Hardware and Algorithms
## Hardware Improvements
- **D-Wave Advantage System:** Features over 5,000 qubits.
- **Pegasus Topology:** Increases qubit connectivity from 6 to 15.
- **Impact:** Higher connectivity streamlines the embedding process (mapping variables to physical qubits). For dense problems, this preserves the problem structure more effectively and increases the probability of finding high-quality solutions.
## Software and Algorithmic Improvements
- **QUBO Decomposition (QBSolv):** Splits large QUBO matrices into smaller sub-QUBOs.
- **Hybrid Algorithms (e.g., Leap Hybrid Sampler):** Integrates quantum and classical approaches.
- **Impact:** Allows the system to solve QUBO matrices with dimensions that exceed the physical qubit limit of the QA hardware.
## Benchmarking Rationale
The combination of enhanced topology and decomposition algorithms makes it viable to test QA on high-dimensional, dense QUBO matrices. This establishes the foundation for comparing QA against classical solvers in real-world optimization scenarios.

# Scalability Benchmarks (up to 10,000 variables)
## Classical Solvers: Failure at Scale
- Classical methods (IP, SA, SD, TS) degrade in accuracy and become computationally intractable for large, dense problems.
- PT-ICM was excluded entirely due to extreme runtimes.
- **The IP Optimality Gap:** Integer Programming (IP) cannot practically prove global optimality for these matrices. For a size of n=7000, IP left a 17.73% optimality gap even after a 2-hour runtime limit.
## Time Complexity & Performance
- **Runtimes:** Classical runtimes scale poorly. Quantum solvers are orders of magnitude faster.
  - *Example (n=10,000):* HQA took ~0.085s, QA-QBSolv took 101s, and classical SA-QBSolv took 561s.
- **HQA Superiority:** The Hybrid Quantum Annealer (HQA) is the only solver that consistently found the best solutions without accuracy degradation across all problem sizes.
## Decomposition (QBSolv) Trade-offs
- **Accuracy vs. Hardware:** Smaller sub-QUBO sizes (e.g., 30) fit the quantum hardware better, yielding higher accuracy per subproblem than larger sizes (e.g., 100).
- **Time Penalty:** Smaller sub-QUBOs create more total subproblems, increasing the overall solve time.
- **Scaling Limits:** Even with decomposition, QA-QBSolv fails to guarantee the highest-quality solution for problem sizes exceeding 4000.

# Solver Rankings and Performance
## 1. Hybrid QA (HQA) - Top Performer
* Consistently identified the highest-quality solutions across all problem sizes without degrading.
* Exponentially faster than all competitors for massive problems (e.g., ~6561× faster than the best classical solver).
* Solved a 10,000-variable problem in just 0.0855 seconds.
## 2. QA with Decomposition (QA-QBSolv) - Runner-Up
* Highly accurate for small to medium problems, but accuracy begins to drop for problem sizes greater than 4,000.
* Significantly faster than classical solvers, though slower than HQA (took 101 seconds for n=10,000).
## 3. Integer Programming (IP) & Simulated Annealing (SA) - Accurate but Unscalable
* Both IP and SA are highly accurate for small problems.
* IP failed to close a 17.73% optimality gap on a 7,000-variable problem even after a 2-hour runtime limit.
* SA struggles to handle problems larger than 3,000 variables without decomposition due to time and memory limits.
* Using decomposition (SA-QBSolv) improves SA's speed but still takes 561 seconds for n=10,000 and fails to find high-quality solutions for sizes over 4,000.
## 4. Steepest Descent (SD) & Tabu Search (TS) - Fast but Inaccurate
* Both solvers exhibited unacceptably low relative accuracy, failing to find optimal states even for relatively small problems (e.g., 2,000 variables).
* While faster than IP and SA, their poor accuracy disqualifies them for large, dense QUBOs.
## 5. PT-ICM - Worst for Dense Problems
* Excluded entirely from the large-scale benchmark (up to 10,000 variables) due to excessively long solving times.
* While PT-ICM works well for sparse matrices, its effectiveness drastically decreases for dense problems.


# Methodological Caveats and Experimental Bias

While the paper concludes that quantum solvers outperform classical ones, the experimental design contains specific conditions that heavily favor D-Wave's ecosystem:
* **Apples-to-Oranges Comparison (Hybrid vs. Pure Classical):** The top-performing solver (HQA) is a proprietary, closed-source hybrid algorithm. Because the exact mechanics are not public, it is impossible to determine how much of the performance gain is due to actual quantum annealing versus highly optimized classical pre-processing.
* **The Matrix Density Trap:** The benchmark exclusively uses fully connected (100% density) QUBO matrices. The authors acknowledge that classical solvers like PT-ICM struggle specifically with dense problems, meaning the test environment was the worst-case scenario for the classical heuristics evaluated.
* **Arbitrary Resource Limits:** Integer Programming (IP), which mathematically guarantees global optimality, was capped at a 2-hour time limit and 240 GB of RAM. The "failure" of IP at large scales (e.g., n=7000) is a result of hitting these practical limits rather than an algorithmic failure.
* **Lack of Classical Tuning:** Classical solvers (SA, TS, SD) were run using default settings (e.g., standard geometric cooling schedules, random initial states). There is no evidence of extensive hyperparameter tuning for 100% dense matrices, whereas D-Wave's Leap Hybrid is a commercially optimized solver.
* **Artificial Bottlenecking via Decomposition Size:** The sub-QUBO size for the QBSolv decomposition tool was strictly set to 30 because it "fit the QA hardware better." This artificially bottlenecks the classical solvers (like SA-QBSolv), which could otherwise process much larger sub-blocks efficiently in RAM.

“Methods to solve a QUBO Various methods have been proposed to solve QUBO problems. For our benchmarking study, we consider seven representative methods: QA, hybrid QA (HQA), integer programming (IP), simulated annealing (SA), steepest descent (SD), tabu search (TS), parallel tempering with isoenergetic cluster moves (PT-ICM). Below, we provide a brief introduction to each of the solvers used in solving combinatorial optimization problems: Quantum annealing and hybrid quantum annealing. QA starts with a superposition state for all qubits, which has the lowest energy state of the initial Hamiltonian (H0). In the annealing process, the system evolves toward the lowest energy state of the final Hamiltonian (also called a problem Hamiltonian, Hp) by minimizing the influence of the initial Hamiltonian. The measured state at the end of the annealing is supposed to be the ground state of Hp, which can be expressed as the following Eqs.38,39: Hðt=taÞ 1⁄4 A t=ta H0 þ B t=ta Hp ð3Þ Here, t is the elapsed annealing time, and ta is the total annealing time. Sufficiently slow evolution from H0 to Hp enables the quantum system to stay at the ground state, which leads to the identification of the optimal solution of a given combinatorial optimization problem3,40. We use D-Wave Systems’ quantum annealer (Advantage 4.1) to solve the problems using QA, and we set the number of reads for QA to 1000 with a total annealing time of 20 μs. Classical or QA solvers often benefit from decomposition algorithms to identify a high-quality solution (i.e., an optimal solution or a good solution close to the global optimum) for large QUBO problems. Hence, the decomposition of a QUBO matrix into sub-QUBOs is very useful when the size of QUBO matrix is larger than the physical volume of a sampler (i.e., QUBO size > physical number of qubits in QA or memory capacity of a classical computer). Simulated annealing. SA, which is inspired by the annealing process in metallurgy, is a probabilistic optimization algorithm designed to approximate a global optimum of a given objective function. It is considered a metaheuristic method, which can be applied to a wide range of optimization problems46,47. In SA, temperature and cooling schedule are major factors that determine how extensively the algorithm explores the solution space48. SD operates by employing variable flips to reduce the energy of a given QUBO through local minimization computations rather than relying on a calculated gradient in a traditional gradient descent algorithm49. This algorithm is computationally inexpensive and beneficial for local refinement; thus, it can be used to search for local optima. This algorithm can escape already visited local minima by remembering those points called “Tabu List” to keep track of moves during the search, aiming to identify high-quality solutions in a large solution space. This algorithm works well for combinatorial optimization problems with small search spaces. However, it can be hard to evaluate neighboring solutions and to maintain and update the Tabu List with increasing problem sizes. The hyperparameter settings for TS are as follows: 1000 reads, a timeout of 100 ms, and “random” initial state generation. Parallel tempering with isoenergetic cluster moves (PT-ICM). PTICM is an advanced Monte Carlo method designed to navigate optimization space, such as QUBO problems28–30. PT operates by maintaining multiple replicas of the system at different temperatures and allowing exchanges between replicas based on a Metropolis criterion. This approach helps lower-temperature replicas escape local minima with the aid of higher-temperature replicas. ICM identifies clusters of variables that can flip without changing the system’s energy28.
