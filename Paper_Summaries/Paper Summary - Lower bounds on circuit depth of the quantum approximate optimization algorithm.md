# Graph Theory Foundations for QAOA Circuit Depth
## Edge Coloring of Simple Graphs
- **Definition**: Labeling $f: E \to [k]$ where each number represents a color.
- **Proper Edge Coloring**: Adjacent edges (sharing a vertex $v$) must have distinct colors ($f(uv) \neq f(xv)$).
- **Edge Chromatic Number ($\chi'(G)$)**: Smallest number of colors needed for a proper edge coloring (chromatic index).
- **Vizing's Theorem**: $\chi'(G) \in \{\Delta, \Delta + 1\}$, where $\Delta$ is the maximum degree of graph $G$.
## Hypergraphs
- **Generalization**: An edge can join more than two vertices ($E \subset \mathcal{P}(V_H) \setminus \{\emptyset\}$).
- **Linear Hypergraph**: Any two edges share at most one vertex.
- **$k$-Uniform Hypergraph**: All edges contain exactly $k$ vertices.
- **Hypergraph Clique**: A collection of pairwise intersecting edges.
- **Proper Hypergraph Edge Coloring**: Edges containing the same vertex must receive distinct colors.

# QAOA Circuit Depth & Hypergraph Edge Coloring for PUBOs
## Cost Operator Decomposition & Parallelization
- **Unitary Mapping**: For polynomial unconstrained binary optimization (PUBO), the cost operator is $U(C, \gamma) = \prod_{m_i \in M} e^{-i\gamma m_i}$, broken into sequence of monomial gates $U(m_i, \gamma)$.
- **Parallelism Rule**: Two monomial operators $U(m_i, \gamma)$ and $U(m_j, \gamma)$ execute in parallel if and only if their supports are disjoint ($\text{supp}(m_i) \cap \text{supp}(m_j) = \emptyset$).
- **Mixer Operator**: Unchanged as $U(B, \beta) = e^{-i\beta B}$ where $B = \sum_{v \in V} \sigma_v^x$.
## Theorem 1: Circuit Depth via Hypergraph Coloring
- **Hypergraph Construction**: Define $H = (V_H, E_H)$ where $V_H$ is the set of variables $\{1, \dots, n\}$ and edges are monomial supports $E_H = \{\text{supp}(m_i) \mid m_i \in M\}$.
- **Theorem**: Every proper edge coloring of $H$ yields a valid quantum circuit. 
- **Minimum Depth**: The shallowest circuit depth is $\chi'(H) + 1$, where $\chi'(H)$ is the hypergraph chromatic index and $+1$ accounts for the mixing layer $U(B, \beta)$.
## Hypergraph Structural Classes & Bounding
- **Linear Hypergraphs**: Any two edges share at most one vertex (equivalent to any two monomials sharing at most one variable). Bounded by results on the Erdös-Faber-Lovász conjecture and Kahn's theorem ($\chi'(H) \le n + o(n)$).
- **$k$-Uniform Hypergraphs**: Every edge contains precisely $k$ vertices (monomials of size $k$). Bounded using results by Pippenger-Spencer and Alon-Kim.
## Hardware Gate Optimization
- **Gate Width Limit ($L$)**: Monomials can be merged (e.g., if $\text{supp}(a) \subset \text{supp}(b)$, then $\text{supp}(a) \cup \text{supp}(b) = \text{supp}(b)$) to optimize hardware constraints up to max gate size $L$.
- **Optimization Program**: Formulated via Equations 13–16 as an integer program to minimize colors $c \in C$ subject to hardware size limits.
- **Derived Definitions**: The *derived graph* and *derived hypergraph* connect variables found in a common monomial.


# QAOA Circuit Depth Bounds for Specific Combinatorial Optimization Problems
## Overview
Mapping combinatorial optimization problems to QAOA via constraint dualization impacts circuit depth based on the resulting graph or hypergraph structure. Fully connected hardware is assumed as the baseline best case.
## Problem-Specific Depth Analysis
### 1. Vertex Cover
- **Formulation**: Constraints are sums of two unique variables and a constant ($\le 1$). Dualization yields monomials with at most two variables.
- **Circuit Depth**: $2\chi(G) + 1$, scaling directly with the maximum degree of the derived graph.
- **NISQ Suitability**: **High** for low-degree, sparse graphs due to shallow circuits.
### 2. Knapsack
- **Formulation**: Maximize value subject to weight capacity $W$.
- **Challenges**: Large $W$ requires many slack/dummy variables ($\delta_{ij}$), creating a complete derived graph with high edge chromatic index ($\ge n + \ln(W)$).
- **Preprocessing**: Bounding total weight using sorted weights reduces required additional variables to $\ln(w_n)$, yielding a depth of $n + \ln(w_n)$. 
- **NISQ Suitability**: **Poor**. Small $W$ instances require quantum resources, but are efficiently solved classically via dynamic programming. Large $W$ instances exceed NISQ circuit depth limits.
### 3. Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP)
- **Formulation**: Route optimization with subtour elimination constraints.
- **Circuit Depth**: $n - 1 + 2N_c$ (where $N_c$ is the number of constraints).
- **NISQ Suitability**: **Poor**. Dualizing subtour constraints introduces a high density of auxiliary variables and complete graph structures, making it intractable for near-term hardware.
### 4. Boolean Satisfiability (SAT)
- **Formulation**: Minimize clause violation indicators $z_c$ for 3-SAT constraints.
- **Variable Degree**: Degree of variable $x_i$ is $d_{x_i} = |\bigcup_{c \in C_{x_i}} c| - 1 + 2|C_{x_i}|$.
- **NISQ Suitability**: **Favorable** when the set of literals is large while individual clause counts and clause lengths remain small, preventing excessive variable connectivity.
## Core Takeaways
- **2-Variable Monomials**: Problems like MaxCut, MaxIndSet, and Vertex Cover yield depths determined by graph edge chromatic index ($\chi'(G) + 1$).
- **Auxiliary Variables**: Problems requiring heavy constraint dualization (Knapsack, TSP) introduce massive connectivity overhead, destroying circuit shallowness and making them poor candidates for NISQ deployment.

# Conclusion & Key Takeaways: QAOA Circuit Depth & PUBO Mapping
## Summary of Mapping Strategy
- **PUBO Transformation**: Arbitrary combinatorial optimization problems map to Polynomial Unconstrained Binary Optimization (PUBO) problems via constraint dualization.
- **Circuit Depth Bounds**: 
  - For problems where monomials contain at most two variables, the QAOA circuit depth is $\chi'(G) + 1$, where $\chi'(G) \in \{\Delta, \Delta + 1\}$ by Vizing's theorem.
  - For problems with monomials of three or more variables, circuit depth is lower-bounded by the chromatic index of the associated hypergraph $\chi'(H)$.
## NISQ Device Suitability
- **Favorable Problems**: Vertex Cover and low-clause/sparse SAT instances feature low-depth circuits and are suitable for near-term experimentation.
- **Unfavorable Problems**: Knapsack and TSP require heavy constraint dualization, introducing a high density of auxiliary variables that destroys circuit shallowness and makes them poor choices for NISQ devices.
## Critical Nuances & Caveats
- **Sparsity vs. Depth**: Sparser constraints reduce individual gate support sizes, but *do not* guarantee shallow circuit depth. For example, MaxCut on an $n$-vertex star graph requires a circuit depth of $n$ because every edge must receive a unique color.
- **Dualization Complications**: Squaring penalty terms $p(x)$ during constraint dualization can inadvertently generate monomials with larger support than the original expressions.

“5 QAOA circuit depth bounds for some combinatorial optimization problems In this section, we review some combinatorial optimization problems and discuss the depth of circuit required for one QAOA iteration of each problem instance. NP-complete problems can be reduced to other NP-complete problems; however, the act of reducing may impact the depth of circuit, which may or may not be desirable depending on the hardware. In each case below, the depth of circuit assumes fully connected hardware, so these scenarios are the best possible. 5.1 Vertex covering A vertex cover of a graph G = (V , E) is a collection S ⊂ V such that for all x y ∈ E, at least one of x or y is contained in S. Finding a minimum vertex covering is classically NP-complete [28] and is written as min ∑ i∈V xi s.t. The derived graph has vertex set V = xi ∪ δi j for i ∈ [n] and j = i with j ∈ [n]. Note that δi j is incident only to vertices xi and x j , as it only occurs in constraints including those variables. Thus, the depth of circuit is 2χ (G) + 1, so the difficulty of covering problems is directly related to the maximum degree of the problem. Graphs with low degree allow for a shallow circuit in one iteration of QAOA, so they should be suitable for NISQ devices. 5.2 Knapsack In the knapsack problem, a collection of objects, xi for i ∈ [n], are assigned a weight, wi , and a value, vi .
